## Turning concentrated sunlight into high-temperature air

As a research engineer at Heliogen, I worked with partners at GE Aerospace Research on a DOE-supported project to develop an additively manufactured silicon carbide receiver that used air as a working fluid. Our goal was to develop the highest-efficiency, highest-temperature solar-thermal receiver using air as a working fluid.

The work covered design, fabrication, laboratory validation, and testing in a solar field. For the formal project scope, see the [2022 DOE document](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2022-03/CX-025604.pdf) for award **DE-EE0009804**.

## My contribution

From 2021 to 2024, I led on-sun test engineering and optical simulation to inform receiver design. This included optical ray tracing, heat transfer modelling, hazard assessment, and large-scale test engineering to bring our prototypes safely up to 1100°C using only the sun. That meant working across teams, skill sets, and worksites to maximize confidence in the solar optical boundary condition and perform tests to meaningfully advance the design.

In parallel, I developed the technoeconomic models to help the team understand what combination of performance and cost would make the technology viable. Throughout the project life cycle, the team used this framework to inform system performance targets and advance the technology into a product.

## From early development to later testing

The technology continued to develop after my time on the project. In July 2026, DOE [reported testing at Sandia National Labs](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/articles/sandia-test-advances-solar-thermal-technology-high-temperature-industrial) in which the HOTSSTAR receiver heated air to 850°C and reached surface temperatures near 1400°C.
